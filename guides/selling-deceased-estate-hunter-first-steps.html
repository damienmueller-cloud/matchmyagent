Selling a deceased estate in the Hunter: first steps

Hunter NSW sellers: who can sell a deceased estate, when probate or letters of administration matter, and first practical steps — with a solicitor, not DIY legal advice.

Short answer: In NSW, the person who can sell a deceased person's property is usually the executor named in the will (after a grant of probate where needed) or an administrator appointed by the court (letters of administration). Get advice from a solicitor or estate lawyer early — especially before you instruct a real estate agent or advertise. This is general information for Hunter / Newcastle area sellers, not legal or financial advice.

General information only, not legal or financial advice. Deceased-estate sales involve probate, title and tax issues that vary by will, ownership and family situation. Check current NSW Government, Supreme Court of NSW and Legal Aid NSW guidance, and speak with a solicitor (and accountant if tax arises) before you sign anything or list a property.

This guide sits alongside What a contract for sale needs before your home is advertised in NSW and Signing a selling agency agreement in NSW: what to check first. For local selling context (no market stats), see also Hunter local market FAQ for sellers and Selling in Newcastle.

Who can sell a deceased estate property in NSW?

Usually only the personal representative who has authority to deal with the estate — the executor named in the will, or an administrator appointed by the Supreme Court of NSW if there is no will or no executor able and willing to act. NSW Trustee & Guardian guidance says the executor administers the estate and carries out the will; if there is no will, the court appoints an administrator.

Beneficiaries and other family members generally cannot instruct an agent or sell in their own name just because they expect to inherit. Ask a solicitor who has authority in your case before anyone signs an agency agreement.

Do you need probate or letters of administration to sell?

Often yes when the property was owned solely by the deceased, or as tenants in common. The Supreme Court of NSW says a grant of probate is required to deal with real estate held solely in the deceased's name or as a tenants-in-common share. Legal Aid NSW says the same: if the deceased owned property in their own name or as tenants in common, you should apply for probate (if there is a will) or letters of administration (if there is not, or no executor can act).

Joint tenancy is different: Legal Aid NSW and the Supreme Court say ownership usually passes to the surviving joint tenant, and a grant is often not needed for that asset alone. Your solicitor (or conveyancer working with the estate lawyer) should confirm how the title is held before you plan a sale.

When can the estate sell or instruct an agent?

NSW Trustee & Guardian's executor guide says that once probate or letters of administration is granted, you can start to administer the estate's assets, which can include selling assets or transferring them to beneficiaries. If you sell assets before distributing the estate, you must get a fair market price — beneficiaries may sue if assets are sold below market value.

Exactly when you may market, exchange or settle is a legal timing question. Do not assume a timeline from a blog or a friend. Ask an estate solicitor when you have authority to instruct an agent and what conditions (if any) belong in the contract. Do the usual NSW selling rules still apply?

Yes. Before residential property is advertised or offered for sale in NSW, a contract of sale must be prepared (by a lawyer or licensed conveyancer) and available as required — see NSW Government selling-property guidance and NSW Fair Trading's sales-contracts page for agents. Before an agent can market or promote the property, you must sign a written agency agreement. Those rules apply to deceased-estate sales just as they do to other residential sales. What are sensible first steps in the Hunter?

Practical early steps (always check with your solicitor):

  1. Locate the will (home, solicitor, bank, NSW Trustee & Guardian, or Supreme Court searches as NSW guidance describes).
  2. Identify the named executor — or, if there is no will / no willing executor, get advice on who may apply for letters of administration.
  3. Speak with a solicitor or estate lawyer early. NSW Government estate pages say it is always a good idea for an executor or administrator to get professional legal advice.
  4. Confirm how the property is owned (sole name, joint tenants, or tenants in common) and protect the asset (insurance, security) while the estate is administered.
  5. Have the contract for sale prepared before any advertising, and only instruct a licensed agent once your solicitor confirms you have authority to do so.
  6. Choose the agent carefully — compare options, check licences, and understand fees. See How to choose a selling agent in the Hunter, Interviewing agents in Newcastle: a comparison checklist and How to check a real estate agent's licence in NSW.

What about tax, CGT or stamp duty?

Do not rely on this guide for tax. NSW Trustee & Guardian notes that executors can have tax responsibilities (including returns for the estate). Legal Aid NSW says get advice from an accountant about a final tax return. Capital gains tax, duty on transfers and related rules depend on facts such as how the property was held and what happens next. Speak with a solicitor and a qualified accountant — we do not give tax figures or stamp duty rules here.

FAQ

Who can sell a deceased estate property in NSW? Usually the executor named in the will (with a grant of probate where required) or an administrator appointed by the Supreme Court. Get solicitor advice before anyone instructs an agent.

Do I need probate to sell a house the deceased owned alone? The Supreme Court of NSW says a grant of probate is required to deal with real estate held solely in the deceased's name (or as tenants in common). Ask your solicitor about your title.

What if the property was owned as joint tenants? Legal Aid NSW and the Supreme Court say it usually passes to the surviving joint tenant, and a grant is often not needed for that asset. Confirm the title with your solicitor.

Do I still need a contract for sale and an agency agreement? Yes. NSW rules still require a prepared contract for sale before advertising residential property, and a written agency agreement before an agent can market it.
